The bedrock: the Regalian Doctrine
Under Art. XII, Sec. 2, 1987 Constitution, all lands of the public domain and natural resources are owned by the State. Land not clearly shown to be private is presumed public, so anyone claiming private ownership must prove it. The State has a dual mandate: it alienates (classifies and distributes) lands fit for private use, and it manages and keeps the rest (forests, minerals, national parks) for the nation. The main exception is native title: land held by indigenous communities since time immemorial is presumed never to have been public land.

Part 1

Land Governance

1

Land Governance

The rules, processes, and institutions that decide who gets land, how it is used, and how conflicts over it are settled.
⏱ ~5 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

Meaning and concept

Put simply, land governance is “the rules of the game and the referees.” In the Philippines, those rules start with the 1987 Constitution:

  • Regalian Doctrine: the State owns public lands and natural resources (Art. XII, Sec. 2).
  • Four classes of public land: agricultural, forest or timber, mineral, and national parks. Only agricultural lands may be alienated (Art. XII, Sec. 3).
  • Ownership limits: citizens may acquire up to 12 ha (by purchase, homestead, or grant) or lease up to 500 ha. Private corporations may only lease, up to 1,000 ha, for 25 years renewable for another 25 (Art. XII, Sec. 3). Aliens cannot acquire private land except by hereditary succession (Art. XII, Sec. 7).
  • Social function of property (Art. XII, Sec. 6) and social justice: agrarian reform (Art. XIII, Sec. 4) and urban land reform and housing (Art. XIII, Sec. 9).
  • Indigenous peoples' rights to ancestral lands (Art. XII, Sec. 5), implemented by IPRA, RA 8371 (1997).
  • Decentralization: LGUs plan and zone land (Art. X; Local Government Code, RA 7160 (1991)).

Importance

Tenure security
Clear rights encourage investment. A title can also serve as collateral for credit.
Fewer conflicts
Clear boundaries and records prevent overlapping claims and violent disputes.
Equity
Gives the landless, farmers, and indigenous peoples fair access to land.
Environment
Protects forests, watersheds, and coasts that belong to the whole nation.
Revenue and planning
Good land records support real property tax collection and sound land-use planning.
Peace and democracy
Disputes over land and resources are a root cause of rural unrest, as the Huk rebellion showed.
PH
Philippine reality
The class reference material (citing LAMP data, 2004) states that about 60% of real property is held without legal titles, and millions of A&D parcels remain untitled. Mandates also overlap: DENR classifies, DENR or the courts title, LRA registers, DAR handles agrarian land, and NCIP handles ancestral land. Untitled land is “dead capital” that cannot easily be sold, mortgaged, or developed.

Objectives

  1. Secure and clear property rights (tenure security).
  2. Fair access to land, especially for the poor (social justice).
  3. Efficient, transparent, and affordable land services (titling, registration, surveys).
  4. Sustainable use and protection of the public domain.
  5. Fair and fast resolution of land disputes.
  6. Integrated, reliable land information: one map, one record.

Part 2

Land Administration in the Philippines

Land administration is “the process of determining, recording and disseminating information about the ownership, value and use of land when implementing land management policies” (UNECE, 1996). It has four functions: land tenure, land value, land use, and land development (Williamson et al., 2010). The Philippine system grew in layers, and each era added something that is still in use today.

  1. Before 1565
    Pre-colonial
    Customary, communal, and unwritten rights under barangays and sultanates.
  2. 1565–1898
    Spanish period
    The Regalian Doctrine arrived: all lands belonged to the Crown. Haciendas and friar lands formed. The Maura Law (1894) was the last chance to register Spanish titles.
  3. 1898–1935
    American period
    The Torrens system (Act 496, 1902), the first Public Land Act (Act 926, 1903), the Cadastral Act (Act 2259, 1913), Act 2874 (1919), and the Friar Lands Act (Act 1120, 1904).
  4. 1935–1946
    Commonwealth
    The 1935 Constitution, CA 141 (1936), social justice programs, and organized settlement.
  5. 1945–early 1950s
    After World War II
    Rebuilding lost records, independence, agrarian unrest, and the first resettlement programs.
  6. 1946–1972
    Third Republic
    Institutional land reform: NARRA, the LTA, and RA 3844; the creation of DAR.
  7. 1972–1986
    Martial Law / Fourth Republic
    PD 27, PD 705, and PD 1529 under the 1973 Constitution.
  8. 1987–present
    Fifth Republic (today)
    The 1987 Constitution, CARP, the LGC, IPRA, DHSUD, and digital titling.
!
Get the timeline right: the Third Republic is NOT the present
The Third Republic began on July 4, 1946 with independence. It effectively ended when Martial Law was declared in September 1972 (Proclamation No. 1081); the 1973 Constitution formally replaced the 1935 Constitution on January 17, 1973. Today the Philippines is in the Fifth Republic, under the 1987 Constitution. Historians differ on when the Fourth Republic began (1973 or 1981). Some course outlines label the last lesson “current times (Third Republic)” because much of today's system, including CA 141, the Torrens system, and land reform agencies, was built or reshaped during the Third Republic. This reviewer therefore treats it as “Third Republic and Contemporary Period.”
2.1

Pre-colonial Land Administration

Before Spain, Filipinos managed land through custom, community, and kinship. There were no titles, but rights were real and recognized.
⏱ ~2 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

Pre-colonial land administration means the customary ways communities recognized, used, and managed land before Spanish colonization (Scott, 1994). It was not the modern system of titles and surveys, and practices differed from one community to another.

Significant characteristics

Customary and unwritten
Rights came from tradition, use, occupation, and inheritance. There were no registries, and boundaries were rivers, trees, ridges, and community memory.
Barangay-based
The barangay was the basic political unit. The datu allocated land use, settled disputes, and protected shared resources.
Communal ownership
Forests, hunting grounds, rivers, and fishing grounds were shared. Land was seen as a gift from the gods or ancestors, so people were stewards rather than absolute owners.
Usufruct
Families held the right to cultivate and harvest assigned plots, while the community kept ultimate control. Abandoned land could revert to the community.
Social classes shaped access
Datu, maharlika (Tagalog nobles; timawa in the Visayas), aliping namamahay (dependents with their own houses and property), and aliping saguiguilid (household dependents with fewer rights). Accounts include Plasencia's Customs of the Tagalogs (1589).
Locally diverse
Lowland, upland (for example Ifugao and Kalinga), and Muslim communities differed. In Mindanao and Sulu, sultanates governed land through custom (adat) and Islamic law.
PH
Living example
In the Ifugao rice terraces, families inherit terraces, while clan woodlots called muyong protect the watershed above them. This customary land management has lasted for centuries.

Why it still matters (changes and legacy)

  • Spain's Regalian Doctrine treated these customary lands as Crown property, which began centuries of indigenous dispossession.
  • Native title: in Cariño v. Insular Government, 212 U.S. 449 1909, the U.S. Supreme Court (on appeal from the Philippines) held that land held since time immemorial under a claim of private ownership is presumed never to have been public land.
  • IPRA (RA 8371, 1997) recognizes ancestral domains, customary law, and native title. NCIP issues CADTs (Certificates of Ancestral Domain Title) and CALTs (Certificates of Ancestral Land Title).

2.2

Land Administration during the Commonwealth (1935–1946)

A transition government under the 1935 Constitution built the Public Land Act, nationalized land ownership, and began social-justice land programs.
⏱ ~4 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

The Tydings–McDuffie Act (1934) set a 10-year transition to independence. The Commonwealth was inaugurated on November 15, 1935 under President Manuel L. Quezon. Japanese occupation (1942–1945) later interrupted its programs.

1. Constitutional foundation: the 1935 Constitution, Art. XIII

  • Regalian Doctrine and nationalization: public lands and natural resources belong to the State. Their use is limited to Filipinos or corporations at least 60% Filipino-owned, and only agricultural public land may be alienated (Sec. 1).
  • Area limits (Sec. 2): corporations up to 1,024 ha; individuals up to 144 ha by purchase, 1,024 ha by lease, and 24 ha by homestead; grazing leases up to 2,000 ha.
  • Expropriation for social justice (Sec. 4): Congress may authorize the expropriation of lands to be subdivided into small lots and sold at cost to individuals.
  • Filipinos only (Sec. 5): private agricultural land may be transferred only to those qualified to acquire public land. This became the basis of Krivenko.

2. The Public Land Act: CA 141 (November 7, 1936)

Commonwealth Act No. 141 is still the main public land law today, as amended. It provides that:

  • The President, on the Secretary's recommendation, classifies public land as alienable or disposable, timber, or mineral (Sec. 6). A&D land is further classified by use (Sec. 9): agricultural; residential, commercial, or industrial; educational or charitable; and reservations for town sites and public uses.
  • Modes of disposition: homestead, sale, lease, and confirmation of imperfect or incomplete titles (judicial or by free patent).
  • Homestead (Sec. 12): a citizen over 18 or the head of a family may enter up to 24 ha and must occupy and cultivate it. Homestead and free patent land cannot be sold or encumbered for 5 years after the patent is issued (Sec. 118).
  • Judicial confirmation (Sec. 48(b)) originally required possession since July 26, 1894. This was later changed to 30 years (RA 1942), then to June 12, 1945 (PD 1073), and is now 20 years (RA 11573).
  • Reservations (Secs. 83–88): the President may reserve land by proclamation (see Topic 3.4).
  • Special protection for lands of “non-Christian” Filipinos: their land transfers needed government approval (Secs. 120–121). This was protective but paternalistic.

The occupation halted settlement and land reform programs, weakened tenant protections, and destroyed many titles, cadastral maps, and court records. This set the agenda for the post-war period.

2.3

Land Administration after World War II (1945 – early 1950s)

Rebuild the records, restore order, and respond to rural unrest.
⏱ ~2 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

Significant characteristics

  1. 1945
    Records in ruins
    Titles, cadastral maps, survey plans, and court records were lost or destroyed, especially in the Battle of Manila. Boundaries and ownership had to be rebuilt.
  2. 1946
    Independence and reconstitution
    The Republic was born on July 4, 1946. RA 26 (Sept. 25, 1946) set a special procedure to reconstitute lost or destroyed Torrens titles from sources such as the owner's duplicate or certified copies.
  3. 1946
    RA 34: the 70–30 sharing law
    RA 34 (Sept. 30, 1946), under President Roxas, amended the Rice Share Tenancy Act. It gave tenants 70% of the harvest when they supplied the labor, work animals, and implements.
  4. 1946–1947
    Parity rights
    The Bell Trade Act and the Parity Amendment (ratified 1947) gave U.S. citizens equal rights to exploit Philippine natural resources until 1974. This was a controversial exception to nationalization.
  5. 1946–1950s
    Hukbalahap unrest
    Landlessness, unfair tenancy, and debt in Central Luzon fueled the Huk rebellion. Land became a national-security issue.
  6. 1950
    Quirino's resettlement agencies
    EO 355 replaced the NLSA with LASEDECO (Land Settlement and Development Corporation). EDCOR (Economic Development Corps, Dec. 1950), under Defense Secretary Ramon Magsaysay, resettled surrendered Huks in Kapatagan (Lanao) and Buldon (Cotabato). EO 376 (1950) abolished the RPA and transferred its functions to the Bureau of Lands.
★
What changed
Land administration shifted from building systems to rebuilding them (reconstitution and resurveys). Land policy became tied to peace and order: resettlement was used both as land reform and as counter-insurgency. Mindanao became the main settlement frontier, which later caused conflicts with Moro and Lumad communities whose customary rights were not recognized.

Presidents: Roxas, Quirino, Magsaysay (1953–57), Garcia, Macapagal (1961–65), and Marcos (1965–72). The 1935 Constitution remained in force.

Persistent problems
Incomplete cadastral surveys, informal occupation before classification, overlapping claims, slow titling, fragmented agencies, landlord resistance, and underfunded reforms.

B. Martial Law and the Fourth Republic (1972–1986)

  • PD 2 (1972) declared the whole country a land reform area. PD 27 (Oct. 21, 1972) began Operation Land Transfer for rice and corn lands. Tenants could own up to 3 ha (irrigated) or 5 ha (unirrigated); landowners kept 7 ha. Tenants received a Certificate of Land Transfer (CLT), then an Emancipation Patent (EP).
  • PD 705 (1975), Revised Forestry Code: land at 18% slope or more cannot be classified A&D, and unclassified land is treated as public forest.
  • PD 1073 (1977): judicial confirmation requires possession since June 12, 1945.
  • PD 1529 (1978), Property Registration Decree: codified the Torrens system. The LRC became the NALTDRA, later renamed the LRA.

vs

3.1

Concept of Land Management in the Philippines

Using and developing land resources so they serve economic, social, and environmental needs, now and for future generations.
⏱ ~3 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

Meaning and purpose

Land management is the process of managing the use and development of land resources in both rural and urban areas. Its purposes are to maximize productivity, allocate land fairly, protect the environment and the public domain, reduce disaster risk, and balance competing uses such as food, housing, industry, and conservation.

Legal foundation: how classification evolved

  1. 1902
    Philippine Bill
    Placed public lands under government control, to be administered for the benefit of the inhabitants.
  2. 1919
    Act 2874
    The Governor-General classified public land as alienable/disposable, timber, or mineral.
  3. 1936
    CA 141
    The President classifies public land (Sec. 6) and sub-classifies A&D land by use (Sec. 9).
  4. 1975
    PD 705
    Introduced a scientific approach to forest classification: land at 18% slope or more cannot be classified A&D, and forest land at 50% slope or more cannot be used for grazing (Sec. 15).
  5. 1987
    Constitution, Art. XII
    Four classes: agricultural, forest or timber, mineral, and national parks. Only agricultural land is alienable. Once Congress fixes the limits of forest lands and national parks, they cannot be increased or reduced except by law (Sec. 4).

Principles of land management

  1. Classification before disposition: no A&D declaration means no private title (CA 141, Sec. 8; Sec. of DENR v. Yap).
  2. Sustainability and intergenerational responsibility (Art. II, Sec. 16; Oposa v. Factoran).
  3. Stewardship and the social function of property (Art. XII, Sec. 6).
  4. Planned zonification: each use is placed where it fits best through the Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP) and zoning ordinance (RA 7160; EO 72, 1993).
  5. Integrated “ridge to reef” management: upland forests, lowland farms, coasts, and municipal waters are managed as one system.
  6. Participation and equity: public hearings on the CLUP and FPIC for indigenous peoples.
  7. Risk-sensitive planning: avoid hazard areas (RA 10121, 2010).

The science of zonification (sub-classifying A&D land)

i
Forest lands and municipal waters
Production forests can be sub-classified for grazing, tree planting, agroforestry, or fishponds and included in the CLUP. In municipal waters (up to 15 km from the shoreline, RA 7160), LGUs share authority with national agencies such as DENR, BFAR, and PPA. This overlap is why coordinated ridge-to-reef plans are needed.
PH
Philippine example: Boracay, 2018
Years of overdevelopment, easement violations, and poor sewerage led to a six-month closure and rehabilitation under a state of calamity (Proclamation No. 475, 2018). It is a lesson in the cost of weak land management.

3.2

Agricultural Productivity

Good land management raises farm output by using the right land, securing farmers' tenure, and protecting farmland from premature conversion.
⏱ ~3 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

How land management drives agricultural productivity

1. Right land, right use
Classify land by capability and suitability (soil, slope, water). Farming steep forest land causes erosion and low yields; prime flat land should stay in crops.
2. Secure tenure
Owner-cultivators invest in their land. Agrarian reform (PD 27, RA 6657) aims to make tillers owners.
3. Support services
Credit, irrigation, roads, extension, and post-harvest facilities, required under RA 6657 and RA 8435.
4. Protect farmland
Limits on reclassification and conversion keep productive and irrigated land from being lost to subdivisions.

Key laws and tools

Law or toolContribution to productivity
CA 141, homesteadOwnership required actual occupation and cultivation, so productive use was a condition of the grant.
RA 3844 (1963)Leasehold replaced share tenancy; land capability surveys; agricultural support agencies.
RA 6657 Sec. 65DAR may approve conversion only when land has ceased to be economically feasible for farming or the area has become urbanized.
RA 8435 (AFMA, 1997)Identifies SAFDZs (Strategic Agriculture and Fisheries Development Zones) and a network of protected agricultural areas (NPAAAD) to be kept for farming.
RA 7160 Sec. 20Caps on LGU reclassification of agricultural land (see below).
RA 11231 (2019)Agricultural free patents can be sold or mortgaged immediately, so the land can be used as collateral for credit.
RA 11953 (2023)Condoned agrarian reform beneficiaries' loans, freeing farmers to reinvest.
★
LGU reclassification limits (RA 7160, Sec. 20)
A city or municipality may reclassify agricultural land by ordinance, after public hearings, only if the land has ceased to be economically feasible for farming (as determined by DA) or has substantially greater value for other uses. The total is capped as a share of its agricultural land: highly urbanized and independent component cities: 15% · component cities and 1st–3rd class municipalities: 10% · 4th–6th class municipalities: 5%. Land distributed to agrarian reform beneficiaries is not affected; its conversion follows RA 6657, Sec. 65.
Reclassification (LGU)
The Sanggunian specifies, through the land use plan, how agricultural land may be used for non-agricultural purposes.
vs
Conversion (DAR)
DAR approval to actually change the use of agricultural land. It is required even after LGU reclassification if the reclassification came after June 15, 1988.
PH
Philippine example
The Sumilao, Bukidnon farmers marched to Manila in 2007 against the conversion of their land to agro-industrial use. The march put the conflict between farmland protection and development in the national spotlight.

3.3

Settlement Development

Allocating, planning, and developing land for homes and communities, from frontier colonies to socialized housing.
⏱ ~3 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

Settlement development is the planned allocation and development of land for human settlements: homes, villages, towns, and resettlement sites, together with their basic services.

How the approach evolved

  1. 1935–1939
    Colonization
    Act 4197 and the NLSA (CA 441) organized frontier settlement in the Koronadal and Allah Valleys.
  2. 1950–1954
    Resettlement and rehabilitation
    LASEDECO, EDCOR, and NARRA (RA 1160) moved the landless and surrendered rebels to Mindanao and Palawan.
  3. 1975–1978
    Urban housing
    The NHA (PD 757, 1975), the Subdivision and Condominium Buyers' Protective Decree (PD 957, 1976), and the Ministry of Human Settlements (1978).
  4. 1992
    UDHA
    RA 7279, the Urban Development and Housing Act: socialized housing for the underprivileged and homeless, and humane rules on eviction and relocation.
  5. 2019
    DHSUD
    RA 11201 created DHSUD, merging the HLURB and HUDCC into one department for housing and land-use planning.

How land is allocated, planned, and developed

  1. Plan: the CLUP and zoning ordinance designate residential zones (RA 7160; EO 72, 1993).
  2. Use public land: townsite reservations (CA 141, Sec. 9(d)) and residential free patents (RA 10023): up to 200 sq m in highly urbanized cities, 500 sq m in other cities, 750 sq m in 1st–2nd class municipalities, and 1,000 sq m elsewhere.
  3. Acquire land for socialized housing (RA 7279) in order of priority: government land first, then A&D public land, idle land, and so on, with private land last. Modes include community mortgage, land swapping, land banking, donation, joint venture, and negotiated purchase, with expropriation only as a last resort.
  4. Apply development standards: PD 957 (protection of buyers) and BP 220 (socialized and economic housing standards).
  5. Provide basic services: water, power, roads, schools, and health facilities in resettlement sites.
  6. Relocate humanely: adequate consultation, notice, and relocation before any eviction (RA 7279).
★
Siting factors for settlement areas
Safety: hazard-free sites, away from flood, landslide, and fault zones, and respecting riverbank easements under the Water Code (PD 1067). Optimization: use marginal soils and save prime farmland. Logistics: connectivity, development cost, and closeness to the beneficiaries' livelihoods.
PH
Philippine examples
Dadiangas (now General Santos City) grew from an NLSA settlement founded in 1939. After Typhoon Yolanda (2013), coastal “no-build zones” in Leyte required large-scale planned relocation.

3.4

Proclamations and Reserve Areas

The President can set aside public land for a specific public purpose. While it is reserved, nobody can own it.
⏱ ~4 min read⚖ 1 case digestNot yet completed

What is a presidential proclamation?

Legal bases for reserving land

  • CA 141, Sec. 83: the President may designate by proclamation any tract of public land as a reservation for government use or for quasi-public purposes such as highways, irrigation, public parks, and workers' villages.
  • CA 141, Sec. 88: reserved land is non-alienable and not subject to occupation, entry, sale, lease, or other disposition until it is again declared alienable.
  • EO 292, Book III, Sec. 14: the President may reserve public-domain land for settlement, public use, and specific public purposes; it stays reserved until otherwise provided by law or proclamation.
  • PD 705 (forest and watershed reserves), RA 7586 (NIPAS), and RA 11038 (E-NIPAS, 2018): protected areas, many first proclaimed and then established by law for permanence.

Types and purposes, with examples

Implications for land ownership and use

  1. Inalienable while reserved: the land cannot be acquired by possession, however long. Titles issued over reserved land are void.
  2. Use is limited to the declared purpose and managed by the designated agency (for example DND, a state university, or the tourism authority).
  3. Prior private rights existing before the proclamation are respected, but they must be proven.
  4. A reservation is not a classification: declaring a tourist zone does not make land A&D (Sec. of DENR v. Yap).
  5. Release requires a new proclamation or law. Only after that can the land be disposed of under CA 141 or other laws.
  6. CARP exemption applies to land actually, directly, and exclusively used for parks, forest reserves, school sites, and similar purposes (RA 6657, Sec. 10).
  7. Forest lands and national parks whose limits Congress has fixed can be changed only by law (Art. XII, Sec. 4).

Key Takeaways

Tick each one when you can explain it without looking.

  1. Regalian Doctrine (Art. XII, Sec. 2): the State owns public lands, and private ownership must be proven. Native title is the main exception.
  2. Governance sets the rules, administration records and implements rights, and management plans and develops land use.
  3. Only agricultural public land is alienable. Citizens may acquire up to 12 ha or lease up to 500 ha; corporations may only lease, up to 1,000 ha (the 1935 Constitution allowed 144 ha by purchase, 1,024 ha by lease, and 24 ha by homestead).
  4. Historical flow: customary and communal (pre-colonial) → Regalian Doctrine (Spain) → Torrens system and public land acts (America) → CA 141 and social justice (Commonwealth) → reconstitution and unrest (post-war) → NARRA, LTA, RA 3844, DAR (Third Republic) → PD 27, PD 705, PD 1529 (Martial Law) → CARP, the LGC, IPRA, DHSUD (Fifth Republic).
  5. The Third Republic is 1946–1972. The present period is the Fifth Republic under the 1987 Constitution.
  6. Classification comes before disposition: a positive act declaring land A&D is required (Yap, RA 11573).
  7. Reclassification (LGU) is not conversion (DAR) (Ros v. DAR). The reclassification caps are 15%, 10%, and 5%.
  8. Expropriation for land reform moved from “large estates only” (Guido, 1949) to social justice and the number of people benefited (J.M. Tuason, 1970; Sumulong, 1987). CARP is a revolutionary expropriation (ASLP, 1989).
  9. Reserved land is inalienable until it is released by proclamation or law (CA 141, Sec. 88).
  10. Sustainability: the State manages land and resources for future generations (Oposa). Transparency and rule of law govern public land deals (Chavez v. PEA).
